# Presentation Playbook

A tactical framework for building presentations that move people to action. Synthesized from nine books and the best practices of speakers like Simon Sinek.

**Sources:** Talk Like TED (Gallo), Speak Like a CEO (Bates), Speak Like Churchill, Stand Like Lincoln (Humes), Stories That Stick (Hall), Never Eat Alone (Ferrazzi & Raz), Influence (Cialdini), The 48 Laws of Power (Greene), Designing Your Work Life (Burnett & Evans), The First 90 Days (Watkins)

---

## Part 1: Before You Build a Single Slide

### Know Your One Thing

Every great presentation answers one question: **"What is the ONE thing I need my audience to believe when they leave?"**

Not three things. Not a theme. One sentence. If you can't say it in 10 words, you don't know it yet.

- **Sinek's Golden Circle:** Start with WHY. Not what you did, not how you did it — why it matters. The WHY is the emotional core that makes people care. "People don't buy what you do, they buy why you do it."
- **Humes' Power Statement:** Churchill never walked into a room without knowing his "Power Line" — the one sentence the audience would remember and repeat. Write yours first. Build everything else around it.
- **Watkins' Early Win Framing:** If you're new to a team or presenting early in a role, your one thing must connect to your credibility arc — what have you learned, what can you now see, why should they trust your lens?

**Exercise:** Write your Power Line. Then say it out loud. If it doesn't land in conversation, it won't land on a slide.

---

### Know Your Audience Better Than They Know Themselves

Before you design anything, answer:

1. **What do they already believe?** (You must meet them there before you can move them.)
2. **What's their emotional state?** (Skeptical? Overwhelmed? Excited? Bored?)
3. **What do they need to decide or do after this?** (Your presentation succeeds only if it changes behavior.)
4. **Who is the real decision-maker in the room?** (Tailor to them without alienating the rest.)
5. **What is their attention currency?** (How many minutes will they give you before checking out?)

- **Cialdini's Liking Principle:** People say yes to people they like. Before persuading, find genuine common ground. Reference shared challenges, shared vocabulary, shared goals.
- **Ferrazzi's Generosity Frame:** Never present from a position of "look what I did." Present from "here's what I found that helps YOU." Every slide should feel like a gift of insight, not a receipt of effort.
- **Greene's Law 4 (Always Say Less Than Necessary):** The less you say, the more powerful you appear. Trim ruthlessly. If a point doesn't serve the audience's needs, kill it.

## Part 2: Structure — The Architecture of Persuasion

### The Three-Act Structure

Every presentation that moves people follows the same skeleton:

**Act 1 — Disruption (10-15% of time)**
Break the pattern. The audience is on autopilot. Your first job is to make them lean forward.

**Act 2 — Revelation (70-80% of time)**
Deliver your insight, evidence, and narrative. This is the body — but it must feel like a journey, not a data dump.

**Act 3 — Resolution (10-15% of time)**
Land the plane. What should they believe now? What should they do? Make the next step crystal clear.

---

### Act 1: The Opening — You Have 30 Seconds

The opening determines everything. A weak opening gives the audience permission to check out. A strong one creates a contract: "This is going to be worth your time."

**Five Proven Openers (pick one):**

| Opener | Example | Source |
|--------|---------|--------|
| **The Startling Stat** | "We scanned 25 tables. Only 6 had unique signal. The rest are noise." | Gallo (TED) |
| **The Question** | "What if I told you we're sitting on data that predicts visits — and we're not using it?" | Sinek (Why) |
| **The Story** | "Last Tuesday at 2am, I was staring at a query result that didn't make sense..." | Hall (Stories) |
| **The Bold Claim** | "Our targeting system works. I can prove it. And I can show you what's missing." | Humes (Churchill) |
| **The Contrast** | "Here's what we thought mattered. Here's what actually does." | Bates (CEO) |

**Rules for the opening:**
- Never start with "So today I'm going to talk about..." That's throat-clearing, not communication.
- Never start with an apology or caveat. ("I know this is technical..." "I didn't have much time...")
- Never start with your credentials. Let the work speak.
- **Humes' Power Pause:** Before your first word, stand still. Make eye contact. Let the silence create gravity. Then speak. Churchill did this. Sinek does this. It signals command.

---

### Act 2: The Body — Build a Narrative, Not a Report

**The Cardinal Sin:** Presenting findings in the order you discovered them. Your audience doesn't need your journey — they need your destination, told as a story.

**Narrative structures that work:**

**1. Problem → Discovery → Implication (Best for analytical findings)**
- Here's the problem we were trying to solve
- Here's what we found (the reveal)
- Here's what it means for you (the implication)

**2. What → So What → Now What (Best for recommendations)**
- What we observed
- Why it matters
- What we should do about it

**3. Before → Breakthrough → After (Best for showing impact)**
- Here's where we were
- Here's what changed
- Here's where we are now / could be

**4. The Kindra Hall Story Framework (Best for making data human)**
Every story needs four components:
- **Identifiable character** — Someone the audience recognizes (could be a user, an IP, an advertiser, the team)
- **Authentic emotion** — What was the frustration? The surprise? The excitement?
- **A moment** — A specific, concrete scene (not an abstraction)
- **A specific detail** — One vivid detail that makes it real ("at 2am," "row 363,749," "a single Samsung TV in Boise")

**Hall's Four Story Types:**
| Type | Purpose | When to Use |
|------|---------|-------------|
| **Value Story** | Proves the worth of what you're presenting | When proposing a new feature, tool, or initiative |
| **Founder Story** | Explains the origin — why this work started | When giving context to a new audience |
| **Purpose Story** | Connects work to a bigger mission | When you need buy-in beyond the immediate team |
| **Customer Story** | Shows real-world impact | When you need to make abstract data concrete |

---

### The Evidence Layer — Making Data Persuasive

Data doesn't persuade. **Interpreted data** persuades.

**Principles:**

- **One number per slide, maximum.** If a slide has more than one number, the audience will remember zero numbers. (Gallo)
- **Anchor before you reveal.** Give context first: "The current system predicts with 84% accuracy. The question was: how much new signal is out there?" THEN show the number. (Cialdini — anchoring)
- **Use contrast, not absolutes.** "639% increase" is more powerful than "0.598 SHAP." Relative beats absolute every time. (Humes)
- **Round aggressively.** Say "six hundred percent" not "six hundred and thirty-nine percent." Precision signals accuracy to analysts. Rounding signals confidence to executives. Know your audience. (Bates)
- **The Rule of Three.** The human brain processes information in threes. Three takeaways. Three categories. Three next steps. Not four. Not five. Three. (Gallo, Humes, classical rhetoric)

**Cialdini's Persuasion Principles Applied to Data Presentations:**

| Principle | Application |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Social Proof** | "This approach is used by [peer company / industry standard / recognized team]" |
| **Authority** | Cite the method, the scale, the rigor. "363K pairs. 25 tables scanned. XGBoost with SHAP." |
| **Scarcity** | "This data has a 90-day TTL. If we don't act now, it's gone." |
| **Commitment** | Get a small "yes" early. "Do we agree the current system works? Good. Now here's what's missing." |
| **Reciprocity** | Give insight freely. Share the full methodology. Transparency creates trust and obligation. |
| **Unity** | "We" not "I." "Our system." "Our data." Frame as shared ownership. |

---

### Act 3: The Close — The Last Thing They Hear Is the First Thing They Remember

**Three closing techniques:**

1. **The Bookend (Humes):** Return to your opening. If you started with a question, answer it. If you started with a story, finish it. This creates narrative closure — the audience feels satisfied.

2. **The Call to Action (Bates):** Be explicit. "Here are three next steps. I need a decision on #1 by Friday." Vague closes ("let me know what you think") waste the momentum you built.

3. **The Power Line Callback (Sinek/Churchill):** End on your one sentence. The last words linger. Make them count. Churchill ended speeches with single sentences that became history. Your Power Line should be the last thing in the air.

**Never end with:**
- "That's all I have" (deflating)
- "Any questions?" as your final line (makes Q&A the climax instead of your message)
- A new piece of information (confusing — the audience doesn't know what to do with it)

## Part 3: Delivery — How You Say It

### Presence (Bates)

Executive presence is not charisma. It's **the appearance of calm authority under pressure.** It's trainable.

- **Posture:** Stand with weight evenly distributed. Shoulders back, not rigid. Hands visible (never in pockets, never behind back). Lincoln's posture communicated that he had nothing to hide.
- **Eye Contact:** Pick 3-5 people in different parts of the room. Hold eye contact with each for a full sentence before moving. This creates intimacy at scale.
- **Voice:** Lower your pitch. Speak slower than feels natural. Pause longer than feels comfortable. Speed = nerves. Slowness = confidence. Churchill spoke at ~130 words/minute — half the rate of a normal conversation.
- **Movement:** Move with purpose or not at all. Pacing is anxiety. Stillness is power. Walk to a new position when you transition to a new point — it physically signals "new idea" to the audience.

### The Power Pause (Humes)

The pause is the most underused tool in presentation. Use it:
- **Before your Power Line** — silence creates anticipation
- **After a key number** — let it sink in
- **After a question** — let the audience think (don't answer immediately)
- **When you lose your place** — a pause looks intentional; filler words ("um," "so," "like") look nervous

Three seconds of silence feels like an eternity to the speaker and a moment of gravity to the audience.

### Vocal Variety (Gallo)

Monotone kills. Vary three things:
- **Pace:** Speed up for energy and excitement. Slow down for importance and weight.
- **Volume:** Drop to near-whisper for intimacy. Rise for emphasis. The contrast is what creates impact.
- **Pitch:** Lower pitch for authority. Higher pitch for excitement (but not too high — it reads as uncertainty).

### Authenticity and Passion (Gallo/Sinek)

Gallo's research on the top TED talks found one common thread: **the speaker genuinely cared about their topic.** You cannot fake this. If you're not passionate about what you're presenting, find the angle that makes you passionate — or don't present it.

- Connect your material to something you personally find fascinating, important, or urgent
- Share what surprised YOU in the work. Genuine surprise is contagious.
- Admit what you don't know. Vulnerability (when paired with competence) builds trust faster than perfection.

## Part 4: Slides — Visual Persuasion

### The Anti-Slide Principles

- **One idea per slide.** If you need a second idea, make a second slide. Slides are free. Attention is not.
- **Kill bullet points.** Replace them with a single sentence, a single image, or a single number. Bullet points are a crutch for the speaker, not a tool for the audience.
- **The Billboard Test (Gallo):** If someone driving by at 65mph couldn't grasp your slide, it has too much on it.
- **Images > Words.** The brain processes images 60,000x faster than text. A single photograph, diagram, or visualization will be remembered when your bullet points are forgotten.
- **Dark backgrounds, light text** for presentation rooms. **Light backgrounds, dark text** for documents that will be read later.

### When You Must Show Data

- Highlight the ONE number that matters. Grey out or remove everything else.
- Use color to direct attention — one accent color for the insight, muted tones for context.
- Label the insight on the chart. Don't make the audience decode it. "This is the moment where NEW features jump 639%."
- Animate reveals when possible. Show the baseline, THEN the change. The sequence creates drama.

## Part 5: Strategic Communication — The Meta-Game

### Positioning Yourself (Watkins / Greene / Ferrazzi)

Presentations are not just about information transfer. They are **reputation events.** Every time you present, you are either building or eroding your professional brand.

**Watkins' First 90 Days Principles:**
- **Secure early wins.** Present findings that are immediately useful — not "interesting" but actionable. The fastest way to build credibility is to solve a problem someone cares about.
- **Adapt to the culture.** Match the formality, vocabulary, and pace of your audience. If leadership speaks in business outcomes, don't present in SQL column names.
- **Build coalitions before you present.** Share your key findings with 2-3 stakeholders before the meeting. No one should be surprised. Pre-selling makes the room feel like consensus, not persuasion.

**Greene's Power Laws for Presenters:**
- **Law 4 — Say Less Than Necessary.** The urge to over-explain is the enemy. When you've made your point, stop talking. Silence after a strong point amplifies it. Words after a strong point dilute it.
- **Law 6 — Court Attention at All Costs.** Not through gimmicks — through surprise. One unexpected insight is worth ten expected ones. Lead with what's counterintuitive.
- **Law 9 — Win Through Actions, Never Argument.** Don't argue that your approach is right. Show the results. Data presented well is more convincing than the most eloquent argument.
- **Law 28 — Enter Action with Boldness.** Hedging ("I think maybe this might suggest...") destroys impact. State your findings directly. "Device diversity is the strongest new predictor. SHAP jumps 639% when we remove existing features."
- **Law 34 — Be Royal in Your Fashion.** Carry yourself as if your findings matter — because they do. If you don't believe your work is important, no one else will either.

**Ferrazzi's Relationship Principles:**
- **Give before you ask.** Before you request resources, budget, or headcount — give insight, knowledge, and solutions freely. Build a reputation as the person who makes others smarter.
- **Follow up.** After every presentation, send a one-paragraph summary to attendees. Include the Power Line and next steps. This cements your message and shows professionalism.
- **Build your network through your work.** Every presentation is a chance to connect with people who care about what you care about. Use it.

### Burnett & Evans — Design Thinking for Presentations

- **Reframe the problem.** If you're presenting "feature importance rankings," reframe it as "what data should we use to find better customers?" Same content, different energy.
- **Prototype and iterate.** Never present a deck for the first time in the real meeting. Rehearse with one person. Get feedback. Adjust. The first draft is never the best draft.
- **Bias to action.** End every presentation with a prototype-able next step. Not "we should explore X" but "I'll have results on X by Friday — who wants to review them?"

## Part 6: The Checklist

Use before every presentation.

### 48 Hours Before
- [ ] Power Line written and memorized (one sentence, 10 words or fewer)
- [ ] Opening rehearsed (first 30 seconds — know it cold)
- [ ] Close rehearsed (last 30 seconds — know it cold)
- [ ] Key stakeholders pre-briefed (no surprises in the room)
- [ ] Slides pass the Billboard Test (one idea per slide)
- [ ] Every data point has context (anchored, contrasted, rounded)
- [ ] Rule of Three applied (3 takeaways, 3 categories, 3 next steps)
- [ ] Story included (at least one — with character, emotion, moment, detail)
- [ ] "So what?" answered for every section (why should the audience care?)
- [ ] Timed the presentation (cut 20% — you always run long)

### 5 Minutes Before
- [ ] Technology tested (screen, projector, remote, backup PDF)
- [ ] Water nearby
- [ ] First sentence in your head (not on a note card — in your head)
- [ ] Power Pause planned (stand, breathe, make eye contact, then begin)
- [ ] Phone silenced

### During
- [ ] Started with disruption, not throat-clearing
- [ ] Maintained eye contact (3-5 people, full sentences)
- [ ] Paused after key points (3 full seconds)
- [ ] Spoke to the audience, not the slides
- [ ] Used "we" more than "I"
- [ ] Ended on the Power Line, not "any questions?"

### After
- [ ] Sent one-paragraph follow-up with Power Line + next steps
- [ ] Noted what landed and what didn't (for next time)
- [ ] Thanked anyone who contributed or gave feedback

## Part 8: Data Visualization — Tufte Principles

Edward Tufte's work is the gold standard for data visualization at MNTN (team-endorsed by Alex Knorr, Andrew Samaha, and others). These principles govern how every chart, graph, and data visual should be constructed.

### The Core Doctrine: Data-Ink Ratio

> "Above all else, show the data." — Edward Tufte

**Data-ink ratio** = (ink used to display data) / (total ink used in the graphic). Maximize it. Every pixel that doesn't encode data is visual noise that competes with your message.

**Remove by default:**
- Gridlines (or make them nearly invisible — #F0F0F0 at most)
- Axis borders / box frames around charts
- Background fills and shading that don't encode values
- Legends when direct labeling is possible
- 3D effects, shadows, gradients used for decoration
- Redundant labels (if the bar says "184x", the axis doesn't need to say it too)

**Keep only what encodes data:**
- The data marks themselves (bars, dots, lines)
- Labels that identify data points
- One accent color for the key insight, muted tones for context
- Axis labels only when the scale isn't obvious from the data labels

### Small Multiples

Instead of one complex chart trying to show everything, repeat a simple chart across categories. The reader's eye learns the pattern once and then scans for differences.

**When to use:** Comparing the same metric across 5+ categories (e.g., per-advertiser lift, per-vertical performance). Instead of one 15-bar horizontal chart, consider a grid of 15 mini-bar charts.

**Rules:**
- Same scale across all panels (the whole point is visual comparison)
- Minimal labels — label only the first panel's axes, let the others inherit
- Sort by the insight (highest to lowest, or most surprising to least)

### Sparklines

Tiny, word-sized graphics embedded in text or tables. Show trend without taking up a slide.

**When to use:** Dense comparison tables, trend summaries, anywhere a full chart would be overkill but a number alone loses the shape of the data.

### Layering and Separation

Tufte uses visual layers to create depth without clutter:
- **Foreground:** The data you're highlighting (bold color, larger marks)
- **Midground:** Context data (muted, smaller, thinner lines)
- **Background:** Reference lines, grids — nearly invisible but structurally useful

This is the Tufte version of "one accent color for the insight."

### The Lie Factor

Lie Factor = (size of effect shown in graphic) / (size of effect in data). Should be 1.0. Never stretch or compress axes to exaggerate effects. If the data has a 184x effect, you don't need to cheat — just show it.

### Chartjunk Checklist

Before finalizing any visualization, ask:
1. Can I remove this element without losing information? → Remove it
2. Does this color encode data or is it decorative? → If decorative, make it gray or remove
3. Am I using 3D, shadows, or gradients? → Remove them
4. Could I replace this chart with a table and lose nothing? → The chart isn't earning its space
5. Would Tufte's Napoleon's March chart be proud of this? → Encode more variables, use less ink

### Interactive Visualizations (RevealJS)

For team presentations, consider generating interactive HTML using RevealJS alongside static PNGs:
- **RevealJS** renders markdown as polished, animated slide decks
- Allows progressive reveal (show baseline, then the change — creates drama)
- Hover tooltips can provide detail-on-demand without cluttering the visual
- Team members (Jason Mills, Mike Dolt) are already using this approach via Claude
- Static PNGs remain the fallback for Jira, email, and async review

**When to build interactive:** Any presentation to a live audience (team meeting, stakeholder review). The animation and progressive reveal make the narrative more compelling than static images.

**When static PNGs suffice:** Jira comments, async Slack sharing, documentation, appendices.

### Tufte Principles Applied to MNTN Data Charts

| Principle | Application |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Data-ink ratio** | No gridlines, no borders, direct-label bars instead of using axis + legend |
| **Small multiples** | Per-advertiser or per-vertical breakdowns as a grid, not a single overloaded chart |
| **Color = data** | Red = key insight, navy = supporting, gray = context. Never decorative gradients |
| **Lie factor = 1** | Linear scales. If 184x, show 184x. No log scales that compress dramatic effects for exec audiences |
| **Annotation > decoration** | One-line interpretation on every chart ("Top-5 keywords carry the vast majority of signal") |
| **Progressive disclosure** | Lead with the hero number, detail in appendix. Or use RevealJS animation to build up |

## Part 9: Applying This to Data Presentations at MNTN

You present analytical findings to technical and non-technical stakeholders. Here's how these principles map to your specific context:

### For Technical Audiences (DS team, engineers)
- Lead with the methodology twist (what you did differently)
- Show the evidence chain — they want to see rigor
- Use the Problem → Discovery → Implication structure
- Numbers can stay precise — they'll appreciate 0.842 AUC
- Still tell a story — "we scanned 25 tables and only 6 had signal" IS a story

### For Business Audiences (leadership, cross-functional)
- Lead with the implication ("we found new data that predicts visits")
- Round everything ("84% accuracy," "600% increase," "6 out of 25 tables")
- Use the What → So What → Now What structure
- One number per slide, maximum
- End with business impact: revenue, cost, competitive advantage

### For Mixed Audiences
- Layer it: business headline up front, technical detail available on request
- Appendix slides for methodology — reference them, don't present them
- Use the "zoom in / zoom out" technique: big picture → one specific example → back to big picture

### Making TI-790 Presentation-Ready (Example Application)

Your current TI-790 presentation is a strong analytical document. To make it a *presentation*:

1. **Power Line:** "We found 30+ new features that predict visits — and the best ones come from content data we're not using yet."
2. **Opening:** "We asked a simple question: what predicts whether an IP will visit an advertiser's site? We scanned every table we have. Most of them are noise. But six tables have signal — and half of that signal is data we've never used for targeting."
3. **Reduce the table:** Show only the top 10 features live. Put the full table in an appendix.
4. **Three Takeaways** structure is already perfect — keep it.
5. **Close:** "The current system works. But there are 30 features we're leaving on the table. The richest signal is in content data — what people watch, where they watch it, and on what device. That's the feature store."
