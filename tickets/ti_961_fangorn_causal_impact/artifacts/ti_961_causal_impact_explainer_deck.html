CausalImpact for Fangorn

How the synthetic-control read works · what the numbers mean

Malachi Dunn · Targeting Infrastructure · 2026-05-28

The question

What would the treated tier's visit rate have been if Fangorn never flipped?

We can't observe that — the flip happened. So we build it.

Causal effect = actual − counterfactual. Everything else is machinery to construct the counterfactual.

Two stages

Pre-period: learn how the treated tier's daily visit rate relates to a set of contemporaneous control signals (other tiers' rate, holiday flag, own lags).

Post-period: we still observe those controls. Run them through the learned model to predict what the treated tier would have done. That prediction is the counterfactual.

The model

Implementation: statsmodels.UnobservedComponents(level="local level", exog=…)

Three pieces:

1. Local level — random walk capturing slow, unexplained drift in the treated tier's rate
2. Exogenous regressors — covariates the data selected (next slide)
3. Observation noise — daily noise around the structured prediction
Fit by MLE on pre-period only. No post-period data ever touches parameter estimates.

6 candidate covariates

Tier × day grain. We don't hand-pick — the data chooses.

Step 1 — VIF

Variance Inflation Factor. Iteratively drop the most multicollinear covariate until all VIF < 10.

Multicollinear covariates can't both stay in the model — they pull regression coefficients in unstable directions and inflate forecast variance.

Example: control_vr and metric_lag1 are often highly correlated (parallel-trends + autocorrelation). One of them gets dropped here.

Step 2 — BIC

Bayesian Information Criterion. Exhaustive best-subset search over surviving covariates, up to size 5.

For each subset: fit OLS on pre-period y, compute BIC = fit penalty + complexity penalty. Pick the subset with the lowest BIC.

BIC's job is to prevent overfitting. A model that fits pre-period noise produces a bad counterfactual. BIC picks the smallest subset that explains pre well.

The chosen subset becomes the exog matrix for the UCM. Selected covariates appear in the bottom of each tier's CI tile.

Aggregating to a single number

Summarize the post-period:

rel_effect = mean(actual) / mean(predicted) − 1
95% CrI = [ mean(actual)/mean(upper) − 1, mean(actual)/mean(lower) − 1 ]

Why relative? Visit rates are tiny (~2%). "+25%" is what stakeholders can act on.

Why average, not cumulative? Cumulative grows with the post-window length — useless for comparing across tiers with different post-period durations.

What the p-value means

"Assuming Fangorn had no effect, how likely is a gap this large or larger?"

p = 2 × (1 − Φ( |mean_effect| / SE ))

SE backed out from the 95% PI width: SE = (upper − lower) / (2 × 1.96).

p ≥ 0.10 is NOT "no effect." It means we can't yet rule out chance given the data. Short post-period, noisy covariates, and a near-zero true effect all produce the same p.

Note: this is a normal approximation. Google's original CausalImpact package uses a posterior tail probability from BSTS sampling. In practice the two agree when the prediction error distribution is well-behaved.

CausalImpact vs DiD

AssumesParallel trendsStable structural relationship

When both agree → strong corroboration. When CI > DiD → CI is "giving credit" for the model's prediction that the treated tier would have followed control's drop. DiD just subtracts the control's change directly.
